# What 1,461 abstracts tell us about ourselves

*A portrait of ASLO-SIL 2026, read from the program*

**1,461 abstracts. 309 sessions. 1,400 unique presenters. 741 institutions. Five days in Quebec City.**

A conference is never only the thing that happens in the rooms. It is also a record of what a community thinks is worth gathering around.

You can learn a lot from who gets a session title, which words keep coming back, which methods have become ordinary, and which topics still have to fight for space.

So I read the program.

Not as a schedule, exactly. More as a self-portrait.

---

## The verbs have changed

The older verbs of aquatic science were often things like *measure*, *describe*, *characterise*. They are still here, of course. We have not stopped measuring things.

But the dominant verbs in the ASLO-SIL 2026 program feel different: *bridge*, *integrate*, *converge*, *scale*.

"Multiple stressor(s)" appears nine times in session titles. "Bridging the gap" appears eight times. "Towards convergence" and "current knowledge application" appear repeatedly. "Cross-ecosystem" frames at least three sessions explicitly.

That is not accidental language. It is the vocabulary of a field that has collected a tremendous amount of evidence and is now asking what, exactly, it can bear.

We do not mostly need more numbers from more places. Or rather, we do, but that is no longer the interesting part on its own. We need to know which numbers, from which scales, support which claims.

The program keeps returning to the same intellectual gesture: putting back together things we spent decades learning how to separate.

---

## Three legacies, and what comes after them

Three tribute sessions in one meeting is a lot.

Mike Pace. Jim Cotner. Jim Elser.

These are not just famous names on a schedule. They are people whose work shaped the grammar many of us still think in: ecosystem process, bacterial production, ecological stoichiometry, long-term questions asked patiently enough that they became infrastructure.

But what struck me was not only that the meeting honors them. It is that the honors sit beside a very visible early-career scaffold.

The program includes:

- **AV001A-D "Amplifying Voices in Aquatic Sciences: Aquatic Confluence"** — four sessions organised by the ASLO Early Career Committee
- **EP010A/B "Sharing Experiences Among Early-Career Researchers"**
- **EC02 "Weathering the Storm: Motivational Tools for Professional and Personal Resilience"**
- **EC03 "Towards a cross-organizational early-career aquatic science alliance"**
- **EP011 "'How to' — Compiling Collective Knowledge to Reduce Barriers for First-Timers"**
- **EP006 "A Decade of Insights with the Raelyn Cole Editorial Fellowship"**

That pairing matters.

The meeting is not only saying thank you to one generation. It is making room for the next one in the same breath. Mike Pace's tribute and a Tuesday-morning resilience workshop sit in the same program because the people who built the meeting think they belong in the same program.

That is not decoration. It is cohort design.

The 2026 meeting is not just honoring its giants. It is handing things off in public.

---

## Where the field actually lives

This is a freshwater-heavy meeting. The SIL half of the joint name is doing real work.

**Lakes / limnology** is the largest single frame, with **405 presentations, or 28% of the program**. Add **rivers / streams** with **126 talks, 9%**, and **wetlands / peatlands** with **23 talks, 2%**, and inland waters carry about 38% of everything said in Quebec.

Marine and oceanography is here, but it is not the center of gravity: **135 talks, 9%**. Estuarine and coastal systems add another 6.5%. Saltwater systems together are about 16% of the program.

This does not read like a marine-dominated ASLO program. The joint SIL meeting and the Quebec venue brought freshwater science back to the front of the room.

Microbial ecology runs through everything: **269 talks, 18%**. So does biogeochemistry: **246 talks, 17%**. These are not separate tracks so much as the working languages of the meeting. Almost every concept-heavy session touches one or both.

The smaller frames are also revealing:

- **Climate change / global change** — 151 talks, 10%. Present, but not dominant in the way it might be at AGU or ESA. This community seems to have internalised climate as a forcing rather than treated it as the whole topic.
- **Indigenous knowledge / equity / community-led science** — **58 talks across 14 sessions, about 4% of the program**, including the striking **EP013 "Two-Eyed Seeing: The Complementarity of Indigenous Knowledge and Western Science,"** **WS05 "Weaving Indigenous Knowledge and Western Science,"** and **WS08 "From Values to Practice: Inclusive Science Spaces."** Not many talks, but many sessions. That is a choice.
- **Disturbance / fire / land-use** — 41 talks, 2.8%. Smaller than I expected, given the stakes.
- **Groundwater / aquifer / hyporheic** — 19 talks, 1.3%. Still the aquatic system we mostly do not see.
- **DOM / NOM chemistry** — 47 talks, 3.2%, concentrated in three sub-sessions of SS050, "Ecological Significance of Dissolved Organic Matter." Small, but coherent.

---

## The inclusivity work is in the architecture

One thing I appreciate about this program is that equity is not only handled as a statement of values. It has been given time, rooms, and session codes.

**EP013 "Two-Eyed Seeing"** is programmed as a science session, not as a side workshop. That matters. It treats Indigenous knowledge as knowledge, not as outreach, context, or cultural garnish.

**AV001 "Amplifying Voices in Aquatic Sciences: Aquatic Confluence"** runs across four time slots. Again, that matters. It is not a single symbolic panel. It creates a recurring space in the meeting, and the speaker list broadens the institutional map of the conference in a way the main corpus does not.

The talks come disproportionately from early-career researchers and from institutions in Latin America, Africa, and South-East Asia: the Philippines, Kenya, Argentina, Brazil. These are places underrepresented in the broader program, where the US at 29% and Canada at 25% together account for more than half of presenters.

There is still a lot to notice here. The global distribution of aquatic science is not the same thing as the global distribution of aquatic systems, or aquatic problems.

But the program is trying, in visible ways, to widen the room.

## The bets being placed

A program is also a set of bets on what people think will matter next.

Some of the bets in 2026:

- **AI/ML in aquatic sciences** is now visible. **35 talks, 2.4%**, tag machine learning, deep learning, or AI. Nandita Basu's POSEIDON platform at Waterloo is one I would watch: a watershed-to-waterway nutrient intelligence platform that treats data, models, and AI as one integrated stack. SS070, "Exploring the Confluence of Data, Models, and AI," names the bet explicitly. In 2031, I would be surprised if this is not above 10% of the program.

- **eDNA / metagenomics / -omics** appears in **47 talks, 3.2%**. That is modest. ASLO still feels more conservative than ESA or general ecology meetings on this front. The community has not fully internalised molecular methods as standard yet. It will.

- **Long-term monitoring** appears in **131 talks, 9%**. This is one of aquatic ecology's great strengths: decades of data on the same systems, held together by people who kept showing up. The program treats long-term observation not as nostalgia, but as method.

- **Mesocosm-based experimental work** appears in **38 talks, 2.6%**, concentrated in SS082, "Mesocosm-Based Approaches for Tackling Grand Challenges." The community is still making space for experiments at a scale between the bottle and the lake. Good.

- **Mass spectrometry of any kind** appears in **33 talks — about 2% of the program**. Fourteen of those use ultra-high-resolution MS (FT-ICR, Orbitrap, or ultra-high-resolution methods); **seven name FT-ICR explicitly**. The DOM symposium (SS050) holds the densest cluster, but MS work also surfaces in contaminant tracking (SS049), isotope tracing (SS041), HAB toxin work (SS048), and emerging proteomics and lipidomics studies (SS009, SS031) scattered through the program. Even so, the molecular-DOM revolution that has reshaped parts of marine biogeochemistry has only partly arrived here. The methods that earned the field its high-resolution data — tandem MS, MS/MS, van Krevelen analysis — are essentially absent from titles and abstracts. That makes SS050 feel different from the rest of the program: smaller, more technical, and still close to a frontier. Disclosure: I am giving one of those seven FT-ICR talks.

---

## One more thing

The meeting is dense.

**257 oral talks + 232 posters land on Friday 15 May alone** — the heaviest single day of the program.

No one can absorb that. Not really. The schedule asks more of attendees than one human can give it.

So maybe the trick is not to try. Pick three sessions you care about. Attend them properly. Talk to people. Follow the bibliographies later.

This is a meeting that rewards depth, not coverage.

---

## A closing thought

A research community is easiest to read in what it makes time for.

ASLO-SIL 2026 makes time for legacies, early-career scientists, Indigenous knowledge, long-term data, lakes, microbes, carbon, models, and the hard work of connecting scales.

It also shows us what is still small: groundwater, fire, chemodiversity, some forms of molecular ecology, some parts of the world.

That is the useful thing about a program. It does not show us the field as we imagine it. It shows us what we actually made room for.

The portrait I see in Quebec is not a field at rest. It is a field trying to synthesize what it has learned, while deciding who gets to help shape the synthesis.

I am glad to be here.

# Reading the Room at ASLO-SIL 2026: 1,458 Talks and the Shape of a Field

**Erika C. Freeman**

*Leibniz Institute of Freshwater Ecology and Inland Fisheries (IGB), Berlin, Germany*

erika.freeman@igb-berlin.de · ORCID 0000-0001-7161-6038

A conference programme is more than a schedule. It is a record of where a field puts its attention: what it measures, what it argues about, and what it decides is worth showing.

Over five days in May 2026 in Montréal, the ASLO-SIL Joint Meeting: Aquatic Confluence ran 1,458 presentations, oral and poster combined, across 309 session items, drawing about 1,400 individual presenters from some 720 institutions. Organised jointly by the Association for the Sciences of Limnology and Oceanography (ASLO) and the International Society of Limnology (SIL), and held three months after the Ocean Sciences Meeting in Glasgow, this was the freshwater-leaning half of the calendar, a tilt worth keeping in mind (on the SIL-ASLO partnership, see Barouillet and Gros 2026). The metadata behind that schedule (the session titles, keywords, and method tags) is itself data. Read that way, it shows clear disciplinary emphases, several cross-cutting fronts, and one structural gap worth taking seriously.

Two limits frame what follows. This is one programme, not a trend line: without a comparable audit of past meetings I can say what this schedule emphasised, not what is rising or falling. And the unit throughout is the presenting author and the scheduled talk, not the field. Meetings are shaped by who can travel and by who is chosen to present out of often many-author projects, so a senior scientist behind several talks can be invisible here, and an early-career researcher without travel funding may not appear at all. The Supporting Information gives the counting rules, including the three empty or withdrawn listings that bring the schedule's 1,461 slots down to 1,458 real presentations. Read with those limits in mind, the programme still tells a coherent story.

## What the programme emphasised

**Long-term monitoring, not artificial intelligence, was the most-tagged method.** Long-term monitoring turned up in about 9% of talks (n ≈ 131) and ran from microbial ecology and lakes through climate, biogeochemistry, and the cryosphere, surfacing in the abstracts more often than in the session titles. Machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) were present too (about 2.4%, n ≈ 35), but spread thin. The pattern is not that AI is remaking the field overnight; it is that patient, multi-decade observation still does the heavy lifting, with AI mostly in support.

**Equity had a visible, cross-cutting presence.** Indigenous knowledge, equity, community-led work, and citizen science appeared in 41 talks across 18 session items, about 3% of the programme. That bundle is deliberately broader than the single Indigenous, equity and social frame plotted in Figure 1 (n = 28), because it also gathers the citizen-science and community-based work; the Supporting Information gives the rule. Small in number, these talks still did real connective work, appearing beside rivers, estuaries, fisheries, food webs, and DOM chemistry rather than staying in one corner of the schedule. The two-part Amplifying Voices symposium and sessions such as EP013 ("Two-Eyed Seeing") gave these conversations dedicated, well-placed slots; the workshops WS05 ("Weaving Indigenous Knowledge and Western Science") and WS08 ("From Values to Practice: Inclusive Science Spaces"), and the community-monitoring session SS079 ("Community-Based Water Monitoring"), carried the theme further. It is worth being precise about placement: EP013 sat in the meeting's Education and Policy track (the EP prefix), not among the Scientific Sessions (the SS prefix), so the fair claim is that the programme gave equity a visible, parallel home, not that it folded it into the core science. Either way, the conversation was not confined to the margins.

## Where the programme converged

**The vocabulary leaned towards synthesis.** The session titles that recurred most were about crossing boundaries: "multiple stressor(s)" appeared nine times, "bridging the gap" eight, and "convergence" six. The words give it away: a field reaching across ecosystems and frameworks.

**Microbial, biogeochemical, and DOM research shared a visible front.** Sessions such as SS058 ("Microbial-DOM Coupling in Inland Waters") and SS050 ("Ecological Significance of Dissolved Organic Matter") treated microbes and molecules as a single coupled system. This is not to say microbial ecology has folded into DOM: only about one in twenty of the microbial-ecology talks in Figure 1 also carried a DOM tag (14 of 269), so the overwhelming majority of microbial work, 255 talks, carried no DOM tag at all. What stood out was less the volume than the ease with which the DOM-microbe interface was programmed as one conversation. In SS070 ("Confluence of Data, Models, and Forecasts"), long-term observation, mechanistic modelling, and forward prediction shared a room: prediction sat inside the science rather than bolted on after the fact. The broader biogeochemical front extended to SS002 ("Gassy Waters: Gas Fluxes Across the Aquatic Continuum") and the compound-specific isotope work in SS041 ("Novel Stable Isotope Approaches").

**Scale and climate drew sessions together too.** Mesocosm work clustered in SS082 ("Mesocosm-Based Approaches"), and the cold- and dry-systems sessions, SS011 ("Aquatic Sciences on Ice"), SS013 ("Seasonal Confluences: Ecology Under Ice"), SS015 ("Drying Waters: Temporary Aquatic Ecosystems"), and SS019 ("High Arctic Limnology in Rapidly Changing Landscapes"), read across several rooms as parts of one climate-change conversation rather than as niche side-tracks.

**Legacy and renewal sat side by side.** Three career-tribute sessions, for Mike Pace, Jim Cotner, and Jim Elser, shared the schedule with a deep early-career track: the two-part Amplifying Voices symposium, first-timer sessions, and mentorship workshops. By design or by chance, the programme set the careers it was celebrating next to the ones just starting.

## Strengths of the programme

**Women were at least as well represented as men among presenters.** Of the 1,284 presenter names the tool recognised, 682 (53%) were inferred female and 521 (41%) male; the remaining 81 are names it judged unisex and left ungendered, which is why the two shares do not reach 100%. Among the 1,203 it did gender, the split runs about 57% female to 43% male. Among the 310 organiser names it recognised, 170 (55%) were inferred female. Read this as indicative only. Gender was inferred from given names with the gender-guesser database, which is built largely on European and English-language names and handles other naming traditions poorly: it failed on 174 of the 1,458 presenter records (12%), and that failure is not spread evenly across regions, so an international meeting is exactly where the method is weakest. An inferred gender need not match a person's lived gender identity, and the programme carried no self-identified pronouns. The Supporting Information gives the rest. Parity at one meeting is still no guarantee across STEM.

**The programme held together intellectually.** A few large frames carried much of the schedule (Figure 1): lakes, ponds, and reservoirs at roughly 24% (n = 346), with a further 4% (n = 65) tagged to limnology and inland waters more broadly, microbial ecology at 18% (n = 269), and biogeochemistry at 17% (n = 246). Those frames gave the meeting a clear centre instead of scattering it.

## Programme density and the challenge of scale

The back half of the week tested everyone's attention. Thursday and Friday (14 and 15 May) carried the most presentations, about 487 each, though part of that reflects scheduling rather than intensity: these were the meeting's only poster days, so their totals sit well above the oral-only days by construction. On oral talks alone the load was still heavy and evenly split: Thursday held 248 oral presentations and Friday 247, each running alongside roughly 240 posters, so the parallel programme left plenty of scope for scheduling conflicts. That crowding is what breadth at this scale looks like. The real question for future meetings is how to keep the breadth without making attention the scarcest thing in the room.

## Geographic gaps and opportunities

Geography is where the programme has the most room to grow, and where collaboration could most obviously widen. A country could be resolved for 99% of presentations (n = 1,444), from the presenting author's institution and, where the institution did not identify a country, their email domain. The US accounted for about 33% (n = 480) and Canada about 29% (n = 428), so between them the two host-region countries gave about 62% of the programme. Some of that follows from the venue and the host community: Montréal is reachable for much of North America, and the geography of the two organising societies' memberships may partly account for it as well. Cost matters here too, but it is relative; "easy to reach" looks very different to a graduate student or a researcher on soft money than it does from a well-funded lab. Venue and membership explain the size of the US and Canada shares, but not who is missing.

Set against each country's share of the world's renewable freshwater, that contrast is striking (Figure 2). Among the twenty countries holding the most freshwater, Japan and Mexico came closest to matching their share, each within about a third of a percentage point of it. Countries holding vast freshwater were thin on the schedule: Brazil holds about 13% of the world's renewable freshwater but gave about 3.6% of presentations (n = 52); India (3% of global freshwater), Peru (4%), and Colombia (5%) contributed five talks between them; and Russia (10%), Indonesia (5%), and Myanmar (2%) were not detected in the programme at all. Those seven countries together account for about 42% of the world's renewable freshwater. Freshwater share is not a quota, and nothing here says how many talks a country ought to send; it is simply a reference point that makes the shape of the gap visible.

Renewable freshwater is only one lens. Water scarcity, water quality, or freshwater biodiversity would each redraw the map, but they point the same way: much of the world holds far more of its inland waters than of this programme. The deeper driver is not water but who can fund and publish the work.

Read generously, none of this judges a single meeting. It maps where collaboration could grow. The easy levers (travel support, hybrid access, and the choice of host city or regional hubs) sit next to harder ones: how we value international authorship, whether funders pay for first-author opportunities and not just data access, and whether the published record carries work in languages and venues beyond the usual few. Neither set moves far without the other.

## Looking ahead

The 2026 programme got the foundational things right: near gender parity within the limits above, Indigenous and community knowledge given a visible home, and a deep early-career track. Those are worth protecting.

A few gentle opportunities also emerge, offered as a member rather than an organiser, and mindful that a programme this size carries constraints I cannot see. At meetings of this scale, spacing the peak days, longer breaks, or hybrid options could help attendees engage more deeply with the densest stretches. And small cross-disciplinary bridge sessions, such as EP013 ("Two-Eyed Seeing") and the Amplifying Voices symposium (AV001), create visible intersection points out of a handful of slots; a little more cross-track signposting would help more people find them.

The clearest gap is geographic, and it is the one with the most actionable levers: travel support, hybrid access, and how first authorship is valued and funded will shape how much of the world shows up next time. The science is strong; the room could be wider.

## Data and methods

Full methods, including how presentations were tagged, how country was detected, and how gender was inferred, are given in the Supporting Information. In brief, the analysis draws on the public ASLO-SIL 2026 schedule (scraped and audited 11 May 2026; 1,458 presentations across 309 session items, from 1,461 scheduled slots), with tags assigned by a multi-label keyword classifier, so categories overlap and percentages do not sum to 100%. The full inventory, classifier code and keyword lists, and the fully reproducible analysis are available at https://github.com/erikafreeman/aslo2026-conference-landscape.

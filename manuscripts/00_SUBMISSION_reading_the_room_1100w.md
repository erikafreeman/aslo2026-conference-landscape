# Reading the Room at ASLO-SIL 2026: 1,461 Talks and the Shape of a Field

**Erika C. Freeman**
*Leibniz Institute of Freshwater Ecology and Inland Fisheries (IGB), Berlin, Germany*
erika.freeman@igb-berlin.de · ORCID 0000-0001-7161-6038

A conference programme is more than a schedule. It is an artefact of collective attention, a record of what a field measures, debates, connects, and chooses to make visible.

Over five days in Montreal, ASLO-SIL 2026 convened 1,461 scheduled presentations across 309 session items, drawing about 1,400 presenters from some 720 institutions. Read closely, the metadata (session titles, keywords, and method tags) maps where the field is placing its attention. Treating the schedule as a dataset turns up familiar strengths, emerging priorities, and a few structural gaps worth taking seriously. What follows is one participant's reading of the programme's underlying network, and what it suggests about where the field is now, and where it may be heading.

## Unexpected signals

**Long-term monitoring outpaced AI.** The most heavily tagged method in the programme was not artificial intelligence; it was long-term monitoring. It appeared in about 9% of talks, threading from microbial ecology and lakes through climate, biogeochemistry, and the cryosphere. Machine learning and AI were present (2.4%), but spread thin. The signal was not that AI is transforming the field overnight. It was that patient, multi-decade observation still anchors much of the work, with AI in a supporting role rather than the main event.

**Equity was integrated, not siloed.** Indigenous knowledge, equity, community-led initiatives, and citizen science ran through 58 talks across 14 session items, about 4% of the programme. The share was modest, but its place in the network was larger than the headcount suggests. Equity and Indigenous knowledge sat alongside rivers, estuaries, fisheries, food webs, and DOM chemistry as bridge topics, linking sub-conversations that would otherwise stay apart. Sessions such as EP013 ("Two-Eyed Seeing") were programmed as core science, not adjuncts. The conversation ran through the main arteries of the programme, not along its margins.

**Lakes were a convergence point.** They were the largest single category, but their weight was not only numerical. Lakes were also the main arena where microbial ecology, climate change, long-term monitoring, and biogeochemistry repeatedly intersected. In a joint ASLO-SIL meeting, it would be easy to read this freshwater dominance as limnology winning the round. The more useful reading is that lakes are the model systems through which the field is now asking its broadest, most complex questions.

## Emerging frontiers

**The programme leaned hard towards synthesis.** The phrases that recurred most in session titles were about crossing boundaries: "multiple stressor(s)" appeared nine times, "bridging the gap" eight, and "convergence" six. The vocabulary itself signals a field reaching for connection across ecosystems and conceptual frameworks.

**Microbial, biogeochemical, and DOM research increasingly read as a single, coherent front.** Sessions such as SS058 ("Microbial-DOM Coupling") and SS050 ("Ecological Significance of Dissolved Organic Matter") treated microbial-molecular dynamics as tightly coupled systems rather than adjacent subfields. In SS070, long-term observation, mechanistic modelling, and forward prediction shared a room; prediction is moving inside the science itself, no longer a translation layer applied after the fact.

**Legacy and renewal sat side by side.** Three career-tribute sessions, honouring Mike Pace, Jim Cotner, and Jim Elser, shared the schedule with a deep early-career track. More than thirty session items carried early-career framing, about 6% of talks in all, including the two-part Amplifying Voices symposium and dedicated first-timer and mentorship workshops. Whether by design or organic submission, the programme set the careers it was celebrating beside the ones just beginning.

## Areas of progress

**Gender balance looked strong.** Among classifiable presenter names, about 53% were female-inferred; among organisers, about 56%. These estimates carry real limits, including a European-biased classifier and a 12% unclassified rate. Within those constraints, ASLO-SIL 2026 sat close to parity, a benchmark still far from guaranteed across STEM meetings.

**The programme also held together intellectually.** Lakes, microbial ecology, and biogeochemistry anchored much of the schedule, accounting for roughly 28%, 18%, and 17% of talks. Rather than fragmenting the meeting, these few large frames gave the programme a clear centre of gravity.

## Programme density and the challenge of scale

The back half of the week tested the limits of attention. Thursday and Friday (14 and 15 May) were the two heaviest days, each carrying about 490 presentations; Friday alone packed 258 oral talks and 230 posters, making conflicts unavoidable. That density is less a flaw than the signature of a thriving, growing field. The practical question it poses for future meetings is how to preserve breadth without making attention itself the scarcest resource.

## Geographic gaps and opportunities

The geography of participation surfaced the programme's clearest opportunity. A country could be detected for about 78% of presentations. Across the full programme, the US accounted for roughly 29% and Canada roughly 25%, together about 54%.

Set beside each country's share of global renewable freshwater, the imbalance was stark. Japan came closest to parity, at about 1.1% of the programme against roughly 1.0% of global freshwater. Several countries with vast freshwater stocks were thin on the programme: Brazil holds roughly 13% of the world's renewable freshwater yet gave about 1% of presentations, while Russia (10%), Colombia (5%), Indonesia (5%), Peru (4%), India (3%), and Myanmar (2%) did not register in the inventory at all. Together, those seven countries steward roughly 42% of the world's renewable freshwater.

Renewable freshwater is only one lens. Water scarcity, water quality, or freshwater biodiversity would each redraw this map, and the deeper driver is not water but who can fund and publish the work. They converge on the same point: much of the world holds far more of its inland waters than of this programme.

Read generously, this is not a verdict on a single meeting. It is a map for future collaboration. The programmatic levers (travel support, hybrid access, regional hubs) sit alongside deeper ones: how international authorship is valued, how funders underwrite first-author opportunities rather than data access alone, and how the published record represents work in languages and venues beyond the dominant few. Neither scale moves far without the other.

## How the programme clustered

Several coherent tracks ran through the week. Microbial-DOM-biogeochemistry work centred on SS050, SS058, SS002 (gas fluxes), and the isotope-tracing in SS041, where most of the mass-spectrometry talks sat. Long-term monitoring threaded through the abstracts more than the session titles, surfacing across lakes, climate, biodiversity, and the cryosphere. Equity and Indigenous knowledge formed a week-long arc through EP013 (Two-Eyed Seeing), WS05, WS08, and SS079. Scale-bridging clustered on SS082 (mesocosms) and SS070 (modelling and forecasts). The cold- and dry-systems sessions (SS011, SS013, SS015, SS019) read almost as one larger conversation about climate-driven change, distributed across several rooms.

## Looking ahead

The 2026 programme got the foundational things right: near gender parity within those limits, Indigenous knowledge as core science, and a deep early-career track. Those are gains worth protecting.

A few gentle opportunities also emerge, offered as a member rather than an organiser, and mindful that a programme this size carries constraints I cannot see. As the meeting grows, spacing the peak days, longer breaks, or hybrid options could help attendees engage more deeply with the densest stretches. And cross-disciplinary bridge sessions such as EP013 and AV001 punch well above their size; a little more cross-track signposting would help more people find these intersection points.

## Final thoughts

Taken as a whole, the ASLO-SIL 2026 programme shows a community crossing disciplinary boundaries, scaling its methods, pairing long careers with new ones, and treating equity as part of the science rather than an add-on. Those are good directions.

The clearest gap is geographic, and also the most fixable: travel support, hybrid access, and how first authorship is valued and funded will decide how much of the world shows up next time. The science is strong; the room could be wider. My thanks to the organisers who built this one, and to the community that made five days in Montreal worth the trip.

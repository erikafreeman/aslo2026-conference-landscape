# What 1,461 abstracts tell us about ourselves

*A portrait of ASLO-SIL 2026, read from the program*

**1,461 abstracts. 309 sessions. 1,400 unique presenters. 741 institutions. Five days in Quebec City.**

A conference is never only the thing that happens in the rooms. It is also a record of what a community thinks is worth gathering around.

You can learn a lot from who gets a session title, which words keep coming back, which methods have become ordinary, and which questions a field has decided to take on together.

So I read the program.

Not as a schedule, exactly. More as a self-portrait. And what it shows is a field that knows what it is good at, knows what it cares about, and is making space — deliberately and visibly — for what it wants to become next.

---

## The verbs have changed

The older verbs of aquatic science were often things like *measure*, *describe*, *characterise*. They are still here. We have not stopped measuring things — and we shouldn't.

But the dominant verbs in the 2026 program are different: *bridge*, *integrate*, *converge*, *scale*.

"Multiple stressor(s)" appears nine times in session titles. "Bridging the gap" appears eight times. "Towards convergence" and "current knowledge application" appear repeatedly. "Cross-ecosystem" frames at least three sessions explicitly.

That is the vocabulary of a community ready to take on the next class of questions. We have collected a tremendous amount of evidence; now the field is asking what, exactly, it can bear.

The program keeps returning to the same intellectual gesture: putting back together things we spent decades learning how to separate. That is not loss of ambition — that is ambition's next chapter.

---

## Three legacies, and what comes after them

Three tribute sessions in one meeting is a lot. **Mike Pace. Jim Cotner. Jim Elser.**

These are not just famous names on a schedule. They are people whose work shaped the grammar many of us still think in: ecosystem process, bacterial production, ecological stoichiometry, long-term questions asked patiently enough that they became infrastructure.

What struck me is not only that the meeting honors them. It is that the honors sit beside a very visible early-career scaffold:

- **AV001A-D "Amplifying Voices in Aquatic Sciences: Aquatic Confluence"** — four sessions organised by the ASLO Early Career Committee
- **EP010A/B "Sharing Experiences Among Early-Career Researchers"**
- **EC02 "Weathering the Storm: Motivational Tools for Professional and Personal Resilience"**
- **EC03 "Towards a cross-organizational early-career aquatic science alliance"**
- **EP011 "'How to' — Compiling Collective Knowledge to Reduce Barriers for First-Timers"**
- **EP006 "A Decade of Insights with the Raelyn Cole Editorial Fellowship"**

The meeting is not only saying thank you to one generation. It is making room for the next one in the same breath. Mike Pace's tribute and a Tuesday-morning resilience workshop sit in the same program because the people who built the meeting think they belong in the same program.

That is not decoration. It is cohort design. The 2026 meeting is honoring its giants and handing things off in public.

---

## What we work on, every day

If you look past the special-session headlines and ask what most of us actually study, the answer is steady and recognisable. These are the **bread-and-butter topics** of aquatic science in 2026:

- **Lakes and limnology** — 405 talks, 28% of the program. The largest single frame. SIL is doing real work in the joint meeting.
- **Microbial ecology** — 269 talks, 18%. The community that asks who is doing the chemistry.
- **Biogeochemistry and the carbon cycle** — 246 talks, 17%. The connective tissue of the meeting; almost every concept-heavy session touches it.
- **Climate change as a forcing** — 151 talks, 10%. Present everywhere, but rarely the whole topic on its own. The community has internalised climate as a driver to *measure* rather than an alarm to *sound*.
- **Marine and oceanography** — 135 talks, 9%. A clear minority in this freshwater-flavoured year, but unmistakably present.
- **Rivers, streams, and the lotic continuum** — 126 talks, 9%.
- **Biodiversity and biogeography** — 98 talks, 7%.
- **Estuarine and coastal systems** — 95 talks, 6%.
- **Food webs and trophic ecology** — 75 talks, 5%.

That is the core. About **80 percent of the program** lives in those eight topical homes. They are the standing wave of the field.

Method-wise, the bread and butter is just as stable. **Long-term monitoring and time-series** appear in 131 talks (9%) — the methodological soul of aquatic ecology, the thing that distinguishes our field from many other ecologies. **Hydrology and hydrodynamics** appear in 115 talks (8%). **Experimental mesocosms** in 38 (3%). **Optical/fluorescence DOM characterisation** runs through the SS050 sessions. None of these are exotic. They are the ordinary craft of a mature field.

## The problems we're tackling

The session titles tell you what we are working on, but the session *descriptions* tell you what we are actually trying to solve. Reading 309 of those descriptions together, a few problem clusters stand out:

**Carbon — where it comes from, where it goes, and in what form.** SS002 "Gassy Waters: Advances in Understanding and Measuring Gas Fluxes Across the Aquatic Continuum" reframes inland waters as both sources and sinks of CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O. SS050 "Ecological Significance of Dissolved Organic Matter" asks what holds DOM together for thousands of years. Several sessions across the program ask, in different framings: *what is the actual carbon balance of inland waters at the global scale, and how do we close it?*

**Multiple stressors — what happens when threats combine.** Nine sessions name multiple stressors explicitly. The frame is mature: the community is past the era of studying one stressor at a time, and the meeting reflects that. The open question is how stressors *interact* — additively, synergistically, with thresholds — and whether interaction depends on system type.

**Climate-driven change in inland and coastal waters.** Warming, browning, drying, ice loss. "Aquatic Sciences on Ice," "High Arctic Limnology," "Seasonal Confluences: Ecology Under Ice," and "Drying Waters" together carry the cryosphere and drought stories — small in count, sharp in framing. These are systems being reshaped in real time.

**Eutrophication and nutrient loading.** 63 talks. A classic problem with a new layer: nutrient management under altered hydrology and altered climate. "Advances in Nutrient Accounting Approaches" (SS010) names the analytical move.

**Disturbance and recovery — fire, logging, urbanization.** 41 talks. "Wildfires and Aquatic Systems" (SS039) is one of several sessions that ask how disturbance regimes are themselves changing, and how aquatic ecosystems track and recover from them.

**Harmful algal blooms.** Multiple sessions, including SS029 "Harmful Algal Bloom Mitigation and Its Consequences" and SS048 on HAB tracking. The community is asking not only what causes blooms but what mitigation actually does.

**Microbial-DOM coupling.** SS058 makes this its dedicated home, and microbial ecology threads through dozens of other sessions. The shared question: are molecular and microbial diversity coupled in ways that ecosystem function depends on?

**Eco-evolutionary feedbacks.** SS030 "Eco-Evolutionary Feed-Backs in Limnology and Oceanography" makes the bet that ecology and evolution operate on overlapping timescales in aquatic systems, and asks what that does to our predictions.

**Integration across scales.** This shows up everywhere: SS082 "Mesocosm-Based Approaches for Tackling Grand Challenges," SS070 "Exploring the Confluence of Data, Models, and AI," and the many "bridging the gap" sessions all name the same problem from different angles — how do we get from molecular detail to ecosystem flux, from one lake to the global record, from data to decision?

These are not modest questions. They are the questions of a field that has done enough basic science to feel ready to take on the hard, integrative ones.

## Where the field is growing

A program is also a set of bets on what people think will matter next. Some of the most interesting bets in 2026 are still small in absolute count — and that is what makes them interesting. Small numbers in a strong program mean *room*.

- **AI and machine learning** are now part of the conversation. **35 talks, 2.4%**, tag machine learning, deep learning, or AI. Nandita Basu's POSEIDON platform at Waterloo treats data, models, and AI as one integrated stack. SS070 "Exploring the Confluence of Data, Models, and AI" names the bet explicitly. The community is adopting these tools on its own terms — pragmatically, and with care about what they can actually answer. By 2031, this will be a much larger share of the program.

- **eDNA and -omics** appear in **47 talks, 3.2%**. Modest, and growing. The community is integrating molecular tools at the pace its science actually needs them. There is room to push, and signs the push is already underway.

- **Mass spectrometry** appears in **33 talks, about 2% of the program**. Fourteen use ultra-high-resolution MS (FT-ICR, Orbitrap, or ultra-high-resolution methods); seven name FT-ICR explicitly. Most of those concentrate in SS050, but MS work also surfaces in contaminant tracking (SS049), isotope tracing (SS041), HAB toxin work (SS048), and emerging proteomics and lipidomics studies (SS009, SS031). The chemodiversity conversation is a coherent young community within the program. Disclosure: I am giving one of those seven FT-ICR talks.

- **Long-term monitoring** holds at **131 talks, 9%**. Not a "growth area" in the trendy sense — it is the field's enduring strength. The program treats decadal records not as nostalgia but as method. People who kept showing up for decades made that possible. It is one of the things the field should keep being proud of.

- **Mesocosm-based experimental work** appears in **38 talks, 2.6%**, concentrated in SS082 "Mesocosm-Based Approaches for Tackling Grand Challenges." The community is protecting space for experiments at scales between the bottle and the lake — a methodological commitment that has paid off and continues to.

- **Groundwater, hyporheic, subsurface DOM** — 19 talks, 1.3%. A small but real community within the meeting. The subsurface is one of the spaces aquatic science is best positioned to grow into, and there is appetite for it.

## The inclusivity work is in the architecture

One thing I appreciate about this program is that equity has been built into the architecture, not added to the brochure.

**Indigenous knowledge, equity, community-led and citizen science** appear in **58 talks across 14 sessions — about 4% of the program**. **EP013 "Two-Eyed Seeing: The Complementarity of Indigenous Knowledge and Western Science"** is programmed as a science session, not as a side workshop. **WS05 "Weaving Indigenous Knowledge and Western Science"** and **WS08 "From Values to Practice: Inclusive Science Spaces"** complement it. **SS079 "Emerging Directions in Community-Based Water Monitoring"** treats participatory science as the methods talk it is.

**AV001 "Amplifying Voices in Aquatic Sciences"** runs across four time slots. That is not a single symbolic panel. It is a recurring space in the meeting, and it broadens the institutional map: the talks come disproportionately from early-career researchers and from institutions in the Philippines, Kenya, Argentina, Brazil — places underrepresented in the broader corpus where the US (29%) and Canada (25%) together account for more than half of presenters.

That is real work, visibly done. The next chapter is bringing the rest of the program into the geographic and disciplinary range that AV001 and EP013 model.

---

## One more thing

The meeting is dense. **257 oral talks + 232 posters land on Friday 15 May alone** — the heaviest single day of the program. No one can absorb all of that. The schedule asks more of attendees than one human can give it.

So maybe the trick is not to try. Pick three sessions you care about. Attend them properly. Talk to people. Follow the bibliographies later. This is a meeting that rewards depth, not coverage.

---

## A closing thought

A research community is easiest to read in what it makes time for.

ASLO-SIL 2026 makes time for legacies and for early-career scientists in the same breath. It treats Indigenous knowledge as knowledge. It protects long-term observation as method. It honors the bread-and-butter craft of lakes, microbes, carbon, and food webs. It is also reaching — toward AI, toward -omics, toward chemodiversity, toward subsurface and cryospheric systems that were until recently outside our reach.

That is what a healthy field looks like. Strong identity. Confident craft. Room to grow.

The portrait I see in Quebec is not a field at rest. It is a field that has learned a lot, is taking on harder questions on purpose, and is deciding — out loud, in public — who gets to help shape what it learns next.

I am glad to be here.

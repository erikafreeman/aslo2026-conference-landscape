# Supporting Information

**Reading the Room at ASLO-SIL 2026: 1,458 Talks and the Shape of a Field**
Erika C. Freeman

## Methods

**Data source.** The analysis draws on the public ASLO-SIL 2026 schedule, scraped and audited on 11 May 2026. The audited inventory contains 1,461 scheduled presentation slots (oral and poster combined) across 309 programmed session items. Session items are counted per block: a session that ran across several time blocks, or that had both an oral and a poster component, contributes one item per block, so the 309 items collapse to 143 session groups under that rule (SS002, "Gassy Waters", occupies eight items on its own). Every abstract identifier in the inventory is unique. One piece of work does appear twice, once as an oral and once as a poster under two separate abstract identifiers (in SS065), so it is the single exception in 1,458 and does not affect the totals. Three of the 1,461 slots are placeholders marked as empty or withdrawn and carry no abstract identifier or presenter; 1,458 slots therefore correspond to an actual presentation, and presenter-level statistics (including the gender inference) use those 1,458.

Oral and poster status was assigned by room: all poster sessions met in room 517C, and no oral session used that room. Classifying by session code alone would misclassify the two "Late Breaking Posters" sessions (LBP01 and LBP02, 10 presentations each), whose codes do not carry the poster suffix.

**Tagging.** Each presentation was assigned disciplinary, method, and problem tags by a multi-label keyword classifier applied to the session name, the session description, and the presentation title. Tagging is multi-label, so a presentation can carry several tags; categories therefore overlap and percentages do not sum to 100% (Figure 1). Disciplinary shares reported in the text are percentages of all 1,458 presentations. Full keyword lists for every category are provided in the analysis repository. The category list and its keywords were not derived from an external ontology: they were read off the programme's own session titles and descriptions and refined by hand until every category had a clear referent, so they describe this schedule rather than the field at large. No formal validation of tag accuracy was carried out, and the tags should be read as a reproducible summary of the programme's vocabulary rather than as an expert classification of each talk.

**Country detection.** Country was resolved from the presenting author's institution first, and only then from their email address. The order matters, and two earlier versions of this analysis got it wrong in instructive ways.

The rule is: (1) match the affiliation string against a gazetteer of institutions, cities and country names; (2) failing that, use the email's country-code top-level domain, but only for institutional domains, since consumer mail (gmail, yahoo, outlook and similar) reflects a person's mail provider rather than their country; (3) only as a last resort treat a generic .edu or .gov address as United States.

Two failure modes this order avoids. Matching .edu or .gov before the country code assigns every address such as .edu.au or .edu.cn to the United States: that error alone moved 56 presentations. More seriously, resolving country from email or from English country names only makes institutions named in Portuguese, Spanish or French invisible whenever their authors use consumer mail, because "Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais" contains no country name and gmail.com carries no country code. That error was both large and directional: it undercounted Brazil roughly threefold while leaving US and Canadian institutions, which are named in English and use .edu or .ca, fully resolved. Since the essay reports under-representation, an unfixed detector of that kind would have inflated its own conclusion.

A country was resolved for 1,444 of 1,458 presentations (99%). The remaining 14 are institutions with no country signal (international NGOs, "Independent Researcher", the European Commission) and are reported as unresolved rather than guessed. Resolution is of the presenting author only, not of all co-authors, and a count of zero means no presentation was resolved to that country rather than proof of absence.

**Gender inference.** Gender was inferred from presenters' given names using the gender-guesser database (Python package v0.4.0). Of 1,458 presentations carrying a presenter name, 174 (12%) were not recognised by the database, leaving 1,284 recognised names. Of those, 682 (53.1%) were inferred female, 521 (40.6%) male, and 81 (6.3%) were recognised but judged unisex and therefore carry no inferred gender. Shares are reported over the 1,284 recognised names; because the unisex group can enter neither the female nor the male count, those two shares do not sum to 100%. The database is built largely on European and English-language names and classifies names from other naming traditions poorly, so the unclassified fraction is not distributed evenly across regions and the estimate is weakest for an international meeting. Inferred names do not necessarily reflect a person's lived gender identity, and self-identified pronouns were not available from the programme. Gender is reported only as an aggregate distribution, never at the level of any individual.

**Equity bundle.** The figure of 41 talks across 18 session items quoted in the text is the union of two tags: the "Indigenous / equity / social" disciplinary frame and the "citizen science / community-based" method tag. It is deliberately broader than the single frame plotted in Figure 1 (n = 28), because the sentence it supports covers Indigenous knowledge, equity, community-led work and citizen science together.

**Freshwater comparison (Figure 2).** Country shares of presentations are set against each country's share of global renewable internal freshwater resources (World Bank indicator ER.H2O.INTR.K3, 2022), for the top-20 countries by freshwater stock, each expressed as a percentage of the world total. The comparison is exploratory and descriptive: a country's freshwater endowment is a reference point that makes the shape of the gap visible, not a quota or an expected number of talks.

**Reproducibility.** The full inventory, the classifier code and keyword lists, the network and matrix visualisations, and the fully reproducible analysis are available at github.com/erikafreeman/aslo2026-conference-landscape.

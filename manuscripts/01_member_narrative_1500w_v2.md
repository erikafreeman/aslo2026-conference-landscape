# What 1,461 abstracts tell us about ourselves

*A portrait of ASLO-SIL 2026, read from the program*

---

**1,461 abstracts. 309 sessions. 1,400 unique presenters. 741 institutions. Five days in Quebec City.**

Every conference is two things at once — the meeting that is happening in the rooms, and the portrait the program paints of the community putting it on. The first you experience by attending. The second you can read by reading.

I read the program. Here is what it tells us about ourselves.

---

## The verbs have changed

The session names of ASLO 2006 were full of *measure, describe, characterise*. The session names of ASLO-SIL 2026 are full of *bridge, integrate, converge, scale*. "Multiple stressor(s)" appears nine times as a bigram in session titles. "Bridging the gap" appears eight times. "Towards convergence" / "current knowledge application" appears six times. "Cross-ecosystem" frames at least three sessions explicitly.

This is a field that knows it is in a synthesis moment and has, with a kind of collective tact, named that out loud in the schedule.

Twenty years of unprecedented data accumulation have left us with the inverse of the problem we used to have. We no longer mostly need more numbers from more places. We need to know which numbers, from which scales, support which claims. The 2026 program is, more than anything, a meeting designed around that need.

The most-cited intellectual move of the schedule is *integration across scales we used to study separately* — terrestrial-aquatic continua, microbial-DOM coupling, eco-evolutionary feedbacks, watershed-to-waterway connectivity. Every one of these phrasings carries the same shape: *what we used to take apart, we are putting back together.*

---

## Three legacies, scaffolded

Three pioneers are honored with full tribute sessions: **Mike Pace** ("Ecosystem Processes in a Changing World", EP001), **Jim Cotner** ("Curating Limnology with Kindness", EP004, three parts), and **Jim Elser** ("Ecological Stoichiometry and Much More", EP012). Three legacy sessions in one meeting is unusual. It signals something about where the SIL/ASLO community is in its life cycle.

But the legacy honoring is paired — explicitly and structurally — with early-career scaffolding. The schedule contains:

- **AV001A–D "Amplifying Voices in Aquatic Sciences: Aquatic Confluence"** — four sessions organised by the ASLO Early Career Committee
- **EP010A/B "Sharing Experiences Among Early-Career Researchers"**
- **EC02 "Weathering the Storm: Motivational Tools for Professional and Personal Resilience"**
- **EC03 "Towards a cross-organizational early-career aquatic science alliance"** — a workshop, not just a talk
- **EP011 "'How to' — Compiling Collective Knowledge to Reduce Barriers for First-Timers"**
- **EP006 "A Decade of Insights with the Raelyn Cole Editorial Fellowship"** — a fellowship-cohort retrospective

This isn't decoration. The society has built actual structure for it: a cross-organisational alliance workshop, a first-timer scaffolding session, an ECR cohort spanning four time slots. Mike Pace's tribute and a Tuesday-morning resilience workshop sit on the same program because the people who built the program think they belong on the same program.

The 2026 meeting is not just honoring its giants. It is *handing things off in public.* That is a deliberate act of cohort design, and as a community move it is rare.

---

## Where the field actually lives

This is a freshwater meeting. The SIL half of the joint name is doing real work. **Lakes / limnology** is the largest single frame, with **405 presentations (28% of the program)**. Add **rivers / streams (126 talks, 9%)** and **wetlands / peatlands (23 talks, 2%)**, and inland waters together carry 38% of everything said in Quebec.

Marine and oceanography is here but in the minority — **135 talks, 9%**. Estuarine / coastal adds another 6.5%. Saltwater systems together are about 16% of the program. This is not the marine-heavy ASLO of 2010. The joint SIL meeting and the Quebec venue brought freshwater science back to the front of the room.

Microbial ecology runs through everything — **269 talks (18%)** — as does biogeochemistry (**246 talks, 17%**). These two are not separate tracks; they are the analytical sinews that hold the program together. Almost every concept-heavy session in the schedule touches one or both.

The smaller frames are also revealing:

- **Climate change / global change** — 151 talks (10%). Present, but not as the dominant frame it is at AGU or ESA. The community has internalised climate as a forcing rather than as the topic.
- **Indigenous knowledge / equity / community-led science** — 28 talks (2%) and **11 sessions**, including the striking **EP013 "Two-Eyed Seeing: The Complementarity of Indigenous Knowledge and Western Science."** This is not many talks, but it is many *sessions* — a deliberate structural decision.
- **Disturbance / fire / land-use** — 41 talks (2.8%). Underrepresented relative to its scientific stakes; no dedicated session.
- **Groundwater / aquifer / hyporheic** — 19 talks (1.3%). The aquatic system we still mostly don't see.
- **DOM / NOM chemistry** — 47 talks (3.2%), concentrated in three sub-sessions of SS050 ("Ecological Significance of Dissolved Organic Matter"). The chemodiversity conversation is small but coherent.

## A deliberate inclusivity scaffold

It is worth naming clearly what the program does about equity, because it does it with structure rather than rhetoric.

**EP013 "Two-Eyed Seeing"** — programmed as a science session, not as a side workshop. It treats Indigenous knowledge as a knowledge system rather than as a topic of study. This is a meaningful framing decision.

**AV001 "Amplifying Voices in Aquatic Sciences: Aquatic Confluence"** runs across four time slots, co-organised by the ASLO Early Career Committee with Pascal Bodmer (Cornell) and colleagues. The talks come disproportionately from researchers in early-career stages and from institutions in Latin America, Africa, and South-East Asia — Philippines, Kenya, Argentina, Brazil — that are underrepresented in the broader corpus where the US (29%) and Canada (25%) together account for more than half of presenters.

**SS013 "Seasonal Confluences" / SS019 "High Arctic Limnology"** — community-led limnology in northern latitudes, with Indigenous communities co-authoring abstracts.

The community-led/citizen-science approach surfaces in **28 talks**. The fact that the cross-cuts add up to ~10% of the meeting — *and that the cross-cuts are programmed as sessions, not relegated to a single equity panel* — is the most important structural decision in the schedule.

## The bets being placed

A program is also a portfolio of bets on what will matter next. Some of the bets in 2026:

- **AI/ML in aquatic sciences** is now visible. **35 talks (2.4%)** tag machine learning, deep learning, or AI. Nandita Basu's POSEIDON platform (Waterloo) is one I would watch — a watershed-to-waterway nutrient intelligence platform that takes data, models, and AI as a single integrated stack. SS070 "Exploring the Confluence of Data, Models, and AI" is the field naming the bet explicitly. In 2031, this will be 10%+ of the program.

- **eDNA / metagenomics / -omics** is **47 talks (3.2%)** — modest. ASLO is conservative compared to ESA or general ecology meetings on this front; the community has not yet fully internalised that molecular methods are now standard. This will change.

- **Long-term monitoring** is **131 talks (9%)** — the field's enduring identity method. The thing that distinguishes aquatic ecology from many other ecologies is that we have decades of data on the same systems. The program honors this as a methodological commitment, not just a heritage.

- **Mesocosm-based experimental work** is **38 talks (2.6%)** — concentrated in SS082 ("Mesocosm-Based Approaches for Tackling Grand Challenges"). The community is reaffirming experimentation at intermediate scale as the bridge between the lab and the lake.

- **FT-ICR-MS / ultra-high-resolution mass spectrometry / chemodiversity** is **2 talks** in 1,461. The molecular-DOM revolution that has reshaped marine biogeochemistry over the past decade has barely arrived at SIL. This is a frontier the community has not yet crossed — and one of the reasons SS050 (the DOM symposium) feels different from the rest of the program. (Disclosure: I am giving one of those two talks.)

---

## One more thing

The meeting is dense. **257 oral talks + 232 posters land on Thursday 15 May alone** — the heaviest single day of the program. The schedule asks more of attendees than any one human can absorb. That is a feature, not a bug — but it is also a quiet argument for *not trying to see everything.* Pick three sessions you care about, attend them carefully, follow the bibliographies later. The community has built a meeting that rewards depth, not coverage.

---

## A closing thought

A research community is most legible in what it puts in the program and what it leaves out. ASLO-SIL 2026 is a community that is **handing off its giants in public, scaffolding its early-career cohort intentionally, programming Indigenous knowledge as a knowledge system, betting modestly but pointedly on AI and -omics, and reaffirming long-term observation as the methodological soul of the field.**

It is also a community where the freshwater half has finally caught up to the marine half in volume, where biogeochemistry and microbial ecology have become the connective tissue rather than separate tracks, and where the verbs of the schedule have shifted from *describe* to *integrate.*

The 2026 portrait is not the field at rest. It is a field reorganising itself for a synthesis moment it has earned. The bets being placed in Quebec this week will look obvious in five years.

I am glad to be here.
